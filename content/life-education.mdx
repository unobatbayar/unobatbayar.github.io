I keep thinking about what a real life education would look like. Not motivational quotes. Not a TED Talk voice telling you to "believe in yourself." Something closer to sitting across a table and talking through the parts of life that actually decide how things turn out.

I'd treat it as one connected system, not six separate courses:

**Relationships**: who you choose, how you communicate, love, boundaries, conflict, trust, and knowing when to leave.

**Family**: being a decent son, daughter, partner, parent, sibling. Handling expectations without disappearing into them.

**Career**: picking a direction, getting good at something valuable, becoming hard to replace, negotiating, leading, eventually creating leverage.

**Money**: earning, spending, saving, investing, debt, taxes, risk. The boring stuff that compounds either for you or against you.

**Wealth**: which is not the same as income. Ownership, assets, businesses, compounding, leverage.

**Life itself**: health, time, identity, purpose, friendships, environment, failure, aging. Making decisions you can still respect later.

### The first principle

Your life is mostly the result of what you repeatedly trade your time, attention, money, and relationships for.

That's it. You have a finite amount of time. Getting wealthy isn't only about learning how to make more money. It's about learning how to allocate scarce resources without fooling yourself.

A few examples that stick:

Trade all your time for money and you can get comfortable, but you're capped by your hours.

Build a valuable skill and each hour starts to matter more.

Build systems and your output depends less on whether you personally showed up that day.

Own assets and money can produce more money while you sleep (sometimes; not always; don't romanticize this).

Build a business and you might combine people, capital, technology, and systems, with all the mess that comes with that.

Build excellent relationships and opportunities, knowledge, support, and trust compound too.

Destroy your health or your relationships while chasing money, and you've just swapped one form of wealth for another. Usually a worse trade than it felt like at the time.

### Income is not wealth

Someone earning \$200,000 and spending \$210,000 is not wealthy. They're running an expensive lifestyle with good cash flow and bad margin.

Someone earning \$70,000 who saves, invests, avoids stupid debt, slowly increases their earning power, and accumulates productive assets may be much closer to real wealth.

The math of compounding is simple. The hard part is building a life where you can stay invested and keep making decent decisions for a long time, through boredom, through market panic, through the years when nothing dramatic happens.

### People before money

I'd put people first, honestly.

A great partner changes the shape of your whole life. The wrong one can make career, money, and mental peace dramatically harder than they need to be. Friends and family work the same way, quieter but just as real.

One of the more useful questions I've found:

> Who am I becoming because I spend so much time with these people?

Not "Are they nice?"  
Not "Do we have fun?"

What kind of person does this relationship push me toward?

### If you want to get rich

Think in sequence, not slogans:

Survive → stabilize → earn → save → invest → own → compound → multiply → protect → give.

And look at the uncomfortable parts directly:

Why smart people stay broke. Why ordinary people sometimes get wealthy. Why lifestyle inflation quietly eats progress. Why chasing "passive income" too early is often a distraction. Why your first \$10,000, \$100,000, and \$1 million are psychologically different problems. Why ownership matters. Why leverage is powerful and also a good way to blow yourself up. Why getting rich quickly often comes with getting poor quickly. How wealthy people actually think about risk. How to spot financial nonsense. How to choose between a job, a profession, a business, and investing. How love, marriage, kids, and family obligations collide with money. And eventually, what "rich" should mean for you, not for someone else's Instagram.

I don't think you need to know any of this beforehand. You just need to be willing to sit with the fundamentals, question your assumptions, and prefer practical frameworks over vibes.

If something in this is bothering you (relationships, family, career, money, getting rich, or the quieter question of what you're doing with your life), start there. That's usually the real curriculum.
